Implications:
  - Use software as the factory's cheap real-money calibration pack, not its main impact bet. The verifier is money: a first paid customer or paid pre-order within 14-30 days (Stripe: 20% of Atlas startups manage ≤30 days). The ledger records visitors → signups → paid → D30/D90 retention → MRR, never LLM-judge or waitlist counts.
  - Drop 'sell the idea or the pre-revenue code' as an outcome for this pack. Pre-revenue software sells for $150-$20K (Acquire average $7,500; Microns closed ~10% of published listings in 2022), and AI is collapsing replacement cost (MirrorCode: 16K-LoC reimplementation for a few hundred dollars of tokens). Only revenue earns multiples (Acquire median 3.9x profit; Flippa apps 2.4x, SaaS 2.7x).
  - Size the portfolio from base rates. If ~17% of launches reach $1K MRR in 2 years (an upper bound, given RevenueCat's selection), a $1K-MRR success exits for ~$20-35K. The expected value per launch is small and heavy-tailed, so run many sub-$2K POCs with hard kill gates. The RCT evidence (Camuffo et al., n=759) says disciplined testing adds value mainly by killing false positives.
  - Building is not the bottleneck; demand and distribution are. CB Insights puts poor PMF at 43% of failures; the Base44 exit is a survivorship outlier. Spend factory compute on specification, finding a distribution channel, and demand tests before code.
  - Point the generator at the 'import and recombine' template: port a demand-proven product, workflow or research method into an underserved niche, profession, geography or platform. This matches the METR finding that imported ideas beat invented ones, and buyers' preference for proven demand. Target B2B niches with built-in buyer-intent marketplaces (e.g. Shopify App Store: 0% revenue share on the first $1M lifetime) over open-web consumer AI apps.
  - Avoid saturated categories: consumer AI wrappers, photo/video AI, generic utilities, habit and productivity trackers. Monthly subscription-app launches are up ~7x since 2022; AI apps churn 30% faster; AI-related submissions to Acquire.com doubled to 10.5%. Add a dedupe/novelty gate against live App Store, Product Hunt and marketplace listings, not only the literature.
  - Use synthetic consumer panels (semantic-similarity rating reaches 90% of human test-retest reliability on stated intent) only as a pre-filter ahead of paid tests. Purchase-intent screens reward familiar ideas and LLM ideas are homogeneous, so diversify generators across vendors, personas and volume.
  - Treat GitHub stars, Hacker News spikes and waitlists as weak, gameable signals. Fake stars number ~6M and help for under 2 months; Hacker News medians are far below the means. Require a payment or retention signal before advancing an idea.
  - Add a security verifier (auth, row-level security, secrets) before any POC takes user data or payments. Vibe-coded apps have systematic failure modes (CVE-2025-48757).
  - Compliance: the Anthropic Consumer Terms version served to this non-US session includes 'Non-commercial use only' and bans scripted or non-human access except via an API key. Use Max for interactive exploration; run automated pipelines and shipped AI features on the API under Commercial Terms; get legal review before commercialising Max-built outputs at scale. The US terms may differ.

  - AI-powered apps earn 41% more revenue per payer but churn 30% faster (RevenueCat 2026). AI novelty sells once but does not retain.
  - Acquire.com: pricing a listing 20% above fair value draws ~2% serious buyer interest, versus ~92% at 20% below. Price discipline dominates outcomes.
  - The Anthropic Consumer Terms version served to this (non-US) session literally says 'Non-commercial use only. You agree not to use our Services for any commercial or business purposes'. This is material for a UK team planning to build products on a Max subscription.
  - MirrorCode: Claude Opus 4.6 reimplemented a 16,000-LoC bioinformatics toolkit (99.95% of tests) that researchers estimated at 2-17 human-weeks, for a few hundred dollars of tokens. A human baseline reached 42% on a ~2,000-LoC task in 20 hours.

  - The 'Show HN' tag gives no measurable star advantage after controls; posting time matters more.

  - LLM product ideas were 7x more likely than human ideas to land in the top 10% by purchase intent, yet less novel and less diverse. This fits the finding that familiar, recombined ideas score (and sell) better than novel ones.

## idea_markets_ip
- Arrow (1962), information paradox: 'Economic Welfare and the Allocation of Resources for Invention' (NBER, The Rate and Direction of Inventive Activity) [none]: Arrow sets out why raw ideas are hard to sell: a buyer cannot value information until it is disclosed, and once it is disclosed the buyer already has it. Quote checked in the NBER PDF: "its value for the purchaser is not known until he has the information, but then he has in effect acquired it without cost." He adds that this stops being a problem only if the seller keeps property rights in how the information is used. | Numbers: 1962; NBER chapter c2144, 18 pp. | Relevance: This is the theoretical reason a disclosed idea sells for about zero unless something protects it first: a patent filing, a contract signed before disclosure (for example prize terms), or bundling with an artifact the buyer cannot reproduce cheaply. | https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c2144/c2144.pdf
- Gans & Stern (2010), 'Is there a market for ideas?', Industrial and Corporate Change 19(3):805-837 [none]: The paper applies Roth's market-design tests (thickness, low congestion, safety) to ideas. Three properties of ideas get in the way of organised markets: ideas depend on other ideas (complementarity), buyers can copy them (user reproducibility), and one buyer's use can reduce another's (value rivalry). Where copying is easy, 'the first buyer is likely to subsequently become a seller'. Formal IP rights such as patents are what make multilateral trade in ideas possible (Ocean Tomo auctions are cited). The authors also note that patent-auction winning bids were 'at relatively modest prices' and that most knowledge trades are bilateral monopolies. Their most speculative conclusion: 'the most robust markets for ideas are those where ideas are free' (open science, where the reward is priority and reputation). Prizes can work, but only when the problem can be specified in advance. | Numbers: The published article runs to 33 pp.; it treats Ocean Tomo and InnoCentive as 'in their infancy'. | Relevance: This is the framework for deciding what the factory can sell. The main routes are (a) patented ideas, (b) problem-pull prizes where the buyer specifies the problem up front, and (c) open publication that earns reputation rather than cash. | https://academic.oup.com/icc/article-abstract/19/3/805/702346

Implications:
  - Do not build a channel for selling raw ideas. Arrow's paradox, corporate non-confidential intake terms, and 17 U.S.C. 102(b) together mean an idea with no filing, no pre-disclosure contract and no artifact sells for about zero. The saleable unit is (a) a solution delivered under a buyer's pre-committed terms (prize or challenge), (b) a verified artifact such as executed results, code, data or a proof of concept, or (c) a filed IP right.
  - Channels ranked for a budget under $2k per POC. (1) Problem-pull prize and challenge platforms (Wazoku Crowd/InnoCentive, and whatever parallel workers list), where the buyer fixes the problem, award and IP terms before disclosure. This is the only fast cash route; the factory would treat open challenges as domain packs with the challenge's own scoring as verifier. (2) Verified open artifacts that earn reputation, consulting, or a route to a startup or acquisition. (3) Patents only as selective options. Invention-promotion firms and generic idea marketplaces should be excluded: their realized base rate is under 1% of clients net-positive.
  - Patent economics are an option, not income. Official fees are trivial: US micro-entity provisional $65; UK filing with application fee payable later; about £405 of UK official fees through examination. Attorney drafting (about $5k-$15k per application, unverified) and 2 or more years of prosecution dominate. Realised sale value appears mainly for issued US patents with evidence of use, in a market of about $158M a year. The factory should buy patent options (for example a US provisional) only for the top few percent of verified ideas, in domains where claims will be used by large incumbents.
  - Add a 'file or publish' gate to the pipeline. The UK and Europe have no inventor grace period (Patents Act 1977 s.2(4)), and the US gives 1 year (35 U.S.C. 102(b)(1)(A)). Any public release destroys UK and European rights immediately: arXiv, a blog, TDCommons, many prize submissions, or non-confidential corporate portals. Ideas not worth filing can be published defensively for free on TDCommons (posted within 72 hours) to timestamp them and protect freedom to operate.
  - Add an inventorship field to the outcome ledger. For each idea that might be patented, record which human framed the problem, which claim limitations came from that human versus Claude, and what the human selected, modified or reduced to practice. Under the USPTO guidance of 28 Nov 2025 and [2023] UKSC 49, an invention conceived only by Claude has no lawful inventor. Whether 'prompt and select' counts as conception is still untested in court.
  - Pick domain packs where the verifier produces the evidence buyers pay for. The empirical pattern (licensing speeds up after patent allowance, publication cuts licensing delay by about 10 months, evidence-of-use raises price 32-108%) says buyers pay to remove uncertainty. The factory's cheap execution-based verifiers are therefore its real product. Licensing works best where patents are strong and incumbents hold the manufacturing or distribution assets (pharma, chemicals, materials, devices), but those usually need wet-lab validation beyond $2k. Computational domains fit the budget, and UK eligibility for ML inventions improved after [2026] UKSC 3.
  - Set outcome-ledger targets from realized base rates, not ratings. Even university tech-transfer offices with lab-validated, professionally patented inventions mostly lose money (87% did not break even on average over 20 years, and the top 5% of universities took 50% of licensing income). A realistic target is a small number of prize wins or paid engagements, with licensing as a long-tail bonus.

Implications:
  - Don't build the business case on 'instantly selling ideas'. The only sizeable idea-for-cash platform (Wazoku) has about 6 open challenges, excludes AI-only submissions and licenses losing ideas to the seeker. At most, treat it as an occasional human-curated channel with feasibility plans and Gantt charts attached.
  - Money actually paid to AI-assisted or autonomous work in 2025-26 went to executable or formally verified artifacts scored by hidden tests: AIxCC $8.5M, ARC 2025 $127.5k, AIMO3 about $618k, Vesuvius $20k+/month, AHC058 1st place. Each domain pack should therefore target a venue whose verifier is external and executable. Log the prize outcome in the outcome ledger as the realized label, and use it to calibrate the (weak) LLM judges.
  - Budget on progress, milestone and write-up prizes, not grand prizes. ARC, AIMO, the K Prize and the Vesuvius grand prizes were unclaimed or withdrawn, and AIMO3 paid about 28% of its advertised pool. Near-term dated targets: ARC-AGI-3 Milestone 2 (30 Sep 2026), Vesuvius monthly (30 Sep), ARC final (2 Nov) and write-up/paper (8-9 Nov; the ARC-AGI-2 write-up prize is $275k), SAIR Lean Kernel (20 Nov) and Andrews-Curtis (30 Nov), Gemma agent (2 Dec).
  - Many code competitions (ARC, AIMO, Kaggle code comps) forbid internet and API calls at scoring time. Claude can generate ideas and write code during development, but the final artifact must run offline on open weights within 9-12 GPU hours. Add a 'distill to offline solution' stage to the executor for these packs.
  - For impact beyond LLM optimisation at near-zero lab cost, the best current verifiers are free wet-lab programs: Anthropic x Adaptyv (Track 3 open 28 Sep to 31 Oct 2026, no guaranteed testing) and CACHE (about 100 compounds per participant tested free, data public). Implications:
  - Score candidate domain packs on three things multiplied together: whether an execution verifier exists, whether there is a live money channel, and the impact tier. On that basis only three sources qualify now: VCC 2026 (verifier and $175k in prizes, deadline Nov 5), FrontierMath Open Problems and the Erdős problems (verifiers with notability or expert review, but almost no money), and ARIA calls (money, with an Arena verifier in Scaling Trust). Treat the rest (80,000 Hours, the Gap Map, the Climate Change AI catalogue, ML-safety agendas, Gates calls) as seed corpora for generators, not as scoring functions.
  - There is effectively no 'instant sale' channel for high-impact open problems. Money arrives as competition prizes (about $10k-$175k with multi-week cycles), grants (ARIA seeds up to £500k; AI for Math Fund $100k-$1M; Gates up to $500k but individuals are excluded and a deployment base is required), or multi-year mega-prizes that need physical or clinical validation (XPRIZE, Longitude). Plan cash flow and the outcome ledger around prize and grant cycles, not spot sales.
  - Proxy verifiers are gameable and only loosely linked to real outcomes. Four sources show it: VCC changed its metrics after 2025, a simple additive model beat deep learning on perturbation prediction, OCx24 reports a gap between computation and experiment, and Matbench calls itself 'not an endorsement'. Every domain pack therefore needs strong simple baselines, several metrics, held-out tests, and one real-world anchor where affordable. This matches the parallel finding that execution beats LLM judgement.
  - Make literature search (import) a first-class operator. On the Erdős problems it produced 68 findings, and AlphaEvolve mostly rediscovered the best known results before improving a few. That fits the METR finding that imported ideas dominate gains, so the factory should retrieve and recombine before it invents.
  - Log notability, not just 'solved'. Tao warns that absence of progress may reflect obscurity rather than difficulty. Epoch's tiers show AI wins concentrated in the 'Moderately interesting' tier (5 of 22), with only 1 of 3 Breakthrough problems solved, and that one with human collaboration. Without a notability field the ledger will overstate impact.

  - Domain selection: prefer domains with executable or real-outcome verifiers and low classifier friction.   - Safety gate: copy the co-scientist pattern. Screen the goal before generation, screen each hypothesis before dedupe and critic rounds, run a monitor across runs, and keep full logs. Store rationales, not the model's full chain of thought, because writing out full reasoning trips the distillation block.

Implications:
  - Sell verified artifacts, not ideas. I found no primary-source case of raw AI-generated ideas being sold on an idea platform. Every cash conversion from 2024 to 2026 went to an artifact scored by an external oracle: hidden test sets (AIxCC, ARC, AIMO, AtCoder), proof-of-concept exploits confirmed by maintainers (bounties, CVEs), expert-checked or Lean-verified proofs (Erdős), customer KPIs (AlphaEvolve), or clinical endpoints (rentosertib). Each domain pack's verifier therefore sets its sales channel.
  - Rough ranking of channels by speed and cost of turning ideas into money for a Claude Max budget with under $2k per POC. (1) Evaluator-driven optimization for businesses such as logistics, forecasting, genomics pipelines or kernels: weeks, compute cost comparable to ALE-Agent's roughly $1.3k contest run, and proven buyer value, but now competing with AlphaEvolve GA. (2) Security vulnerability discovery with PoC and patch: days, cash per finding, but saturating (curl closure; about 20% duplicates and 20% informative in XBOW's queue). (3) Scored competitions: cash is modest ($5k–$25k per placing) outside DARPA-scale prizes. (4) Open math problems: nearly free with verified high impact, but no cash; best used for credibility and to calibrate the factory's hit rate. (5) Drug, antibiotic and materials ideas: lab-validated precedents exist, but they need partner labs and years, and upfront licensing money is tiny compared with headline value (≤$18M of >$2.5B in Insilico's SK deal).
  - Domains 'more impactful than LLM optimisation' that also have cheap verifiers: industrial optimization code (routing, forecasting, scheduling, variant calling, quantum error-correcting codes), open-source security, and combinatorics or optimization constants. Drug repurposing is the high-impact option that works only if a partner lab runs the assays, as with Co-Scientist and Stanford's organoids.
  - Budget for low base rates and high variance. Expect about 0.6% novel hits in open-problem sweeps, inconsistent contest placements (ALE-Agent 21st, then 1st), and about 1 incorrect standalone Erdős claim for every 2 correct ones. The factory should run many attempts per target, record realized outcomes in the ledger, and never let a single LLM verdict gate an idea.
  - The novelty gate must be a literature and prior-art search, not an LLM novelty score. More than half of AI 'solutions' in the Gemini sweep were already in the literature, and the paper warns of 'subconscious plagiarism'. Rediscoveries still have value as imports, which matches METR's finding that imported ideas drive most gains, but they cannot be sold or patented as new.
  - For any licensing or patent route (US or UK), record which human made the conception contribution for each idea. AI cannot be named as inventor (USPTO guidance of Nov 2025; UKSC Thaler 2023), so outputs with no human contribution may be unpatentable.
  - Coverage gap: my web-search budget ran out partway through, so evidence of an autonomous agent winning Kaggle cash prizes, and of idea-marketplace sales, could not be checked. Absence of evidence here is weak evidence.

Implications:
  - Grants and prizes pay teams to execute, not ideas as such: ACX Grants 2025 funded 6.4% (42 of 654), and every program found asks for a credible team and plan. For the ideas factory, 'instantly sold' means rarely through these channels. Treat grants as funding for POCs, not as a place to sell ideas.
  - Run the factory upstream and keep humans in front. Generate, run a cheap POC with Claude Max plus about $1K-$5K of free cloud credit, then have a named human write and own the application with an AI-use disclosure. This meets UKRI and the joint funders statement (human involvement plus acknowledgement), NSF (disclosure encouraged) and, if confirmed, NIH (originality rule, 6 applications per PI). It also follows the research finding that execution, not judge scores, is what validates an idea.
  - Pick domain packs where a funder's thesis and a machine verifier overlap. (a) AI formal methods and cyber: ARIA Safeguarded AI: Cybersecurity, £20m, batch closes 31 Oct 2026, verifier is machine-checked proofs plus red-teaming. (b) Math and Lean: Renaissance AI for Math seed grants up to $100K, rolling, worldwide. (c) AI-for-science and coordination tooling: Foresight, $30K-$100K, deadline 31 Oct 2026, for-profits allowed.   - Openness requirements conflict with selling ideas. Manifund publishes every proposal; AI for Math requires open access; prize platforms and SFF expect public benefit.
